CONCEPT NOTE First Session of the Global Dialogue on AI Governance 6<U+2013>7 July 2026 I Palexpo, Geneva  Designed Elsewhere, Deployed Here: How African Institutions Can Lead AI  Accountability Before It's Imposed Date & Time:  6 July 2026, 07:00<U+2013>07:45 CEST Organizers:  WINAR Institute for AI Policy and Governance in Africa Background and focus Across East Africa, AI systems increasingly inform decisions with direct consequences for  people's livelihoods: creditworthiness in fintech, triage in healthcare, eligibility in public  administration. The frameworks meant to govern these systems, however, were largely designed  elsewhere. European and American accountability models assume institutional conditions that  do not hold in most African deployment environments: reliable audit infrastructure, dense  regulatory capacity, and mature legal recourse mechanisms. This gap is not theoretical. When an imported governance framework meets an African institution  with fewer resources, thinner audit trails, and different regulatory maturity, the result is often an  accountability vacuum rather than accountability itself. A framework built for a well resourced  regulator does not simply scale down. It fails silently, leaving deployed systems ungoverned in  practice even when a policy exists on paper. This session argues that closing that gap requires two shifts. First, accountability mapping must  happen before deployment, not after harm surfaces, so that responsibility is assigned while a  system is still being designed rather than reconstructed retroactively during a crisis. Second,  contextual validation must be treated as a deployment requirement rather than an optional add  on: a model validated for one institutional environment cannot be assumed safe for another  without testing against local data conditions, local infrastructure constraints, and local  enforcement realities. Drawing on Kenya's AI Bill 2026 and emerging governance practice across East Africa, the session  examines what it looks like for African institutions to move from recipients of AI governance to its  architects, building tools calibrated to low resource institutional environments rather than   adapting frameworks built for different ones. The conversation is not simply about resisting  imported governance. It is about building the accountability infrastructure African institutions  actually need, with African expertise at the centre of designing it. Speakers and Format This session takes the form of a 45 minute fireside chat between Winston Mariku and Ole Gamba,  structured as a direct conversation rather than a formal panel, to allow for genuine exchange on  the realities of AI accountability in African institutional contexts. Winston Mariku is Founder and Chief Executive Director of WINAR Institute for AI Policy and  Governance in Africa, an AI Governance and Strategy Advisor working on accountability  frameworks and contextual validation standards for African organizations deploying AI in finance,  healthcare, and public administration. Ole Gamba brings frontline experience navigating cybersecurity, AI, and digital resilience within  one of Africa's largest technology and telecommunications institutions, Safaricom, offering a  practitioner's view of what governance gaps look like when AI systems are deployed at scale  inside African infrastructure. The conversation moves between framework and frontline: Winston brings the accountability  architecture argument from her UN Global Dialogue submission, while Ole grounds it in the  operational realities of deploying and securing AI systems within a major African institution.  Together, the session examines what it actually takes for African institutions to move from  governance recipients to governance architects, closing with concrete reflections on what  accountability infrastructure needs to look like to survive contact with real deployment  environments. Focal Point/Contact: Winston Mariku, winstonaigovernance@gmail.com

CONCEPT NOTE First Session of the Global Dialogue on AI Governance 6-7 July 2026 I Palexpo, Geneva Pathways to inclusive AI governance: learning from the past, co-building the future Date & Time:  <U+25CF>7 July 2026, 12:00<U+2013>12:45 (CEST) <U+25CF>Room E (120) Organizers:  <U+25CF>Organizers <U+25CB>The Future Society     (TFS) <U+25CB>Future of Life Institute     (FLI) <U+25CB>The Elders     <U+25CB>International Association for Safe and Ethical AI     (IASEAI) <U+25CB>Global Network Initiative (GNI)     <U+25CB>Centre for Communication Governance (CCG) at National Law University Delhi       (CCG) <U+25CB>Multistakeholder Approaches to Participation in AI Governance     (MAP AI) <U+25CF>Member State co-sponsors: <U+25CB>Permanent Mission of Greece to the United Nations Office and other International  Organizations in Geneva <U+25CB>Office of the Special Envoy on Technology for the Republic of Kenya Background and focus AI's cross-border impact demands both international coordination and genuinely inclusive multi- stakeholder engagement. The pace of frontier AI development outstrips the cycles through which  governments legislate, build institutions, and reach consensus. Its most significant risks cross   borders regardless of where systems are built; its benefits are concentrating among a few while  the effects are felt by all. No national framework, however sophisticated, can manage this alone. Meeting this moment requires something the international community has built before but must  now develop at speed: multilateral coordination grounded in geopolitical and socio-technical  realities, and meaningful multistakeholder engagement that draws in the wide range of expertise  this technology demands. This includes three essential constituencies. The Global Majority: advanced AI is a truly global  challenge, shaped by decisions made in a handful of jurisdictions yet affecting every country.  Frameworks built without those countries at the table will lack contextual relevance, legitimacy  and durability. The scientific and technical community: the technical risks of frontier AI are real  and specific, yet still poorly understood by many governance actors. Independent scientific  expertise must inform international processes, not follow them. And, civil society: the impact of AI  on society, the priorities of citizens and the scrutiny of independent organisations are not an  afterthought <U+2014> they are an essential contribution to and condition of trustworthy governance. This side event convenes notable global leaders, technical experts, diplomats and civil society  representatives to look to the past to examine how international cooperation around other global  challenges was managed, and discuss how we co-build AI governance for the future. Drawing on  the first UN Global Dialogue and looking ahead to other key AI governance moments, it seeks to  map out how we can advance from learning to action: identifying key lessons, mapping viable  coordination pathways, and seeding the relationships needed to carry them forward. CONCEPT NOTE First Session of the Global Dialogue on AI Governance 6<U+2013>7 July 2026 I Palexpo, Geneva Building Technical Sovereignty: AI Safety and Security Capacity as a  Governance Foundation in the Global South Date & Time:  06 July 2026, 18:00<U+2013>19:00 CEST Organizers: Asuntos del Sur  Access link: https://us06web.zoom.us/j/83211211214? pwd=kzuNaOZKXV21bMd0RsiGRNYlZlzZbc.1 Background and focus Technical sovereignty in AI rests on a country's or region's actual capacity to understand, audit,  red-team, and govern the AI systems it deploys and depends on. For most of the Global South,  building this capacity is the central condition for participating in AI governance as an active agent,  with real influence over the standards that get set. Co-organised by Asuntos del Sur and the Global Council for Responsible AI, this session brings  together practitioners working on the ground in Latin America and Africa to examine what building  this capacity actually requires: AI safety literacy for policymakers and civil society, security  standards and red-teaming practices adapted to local threat landscapes, privacy-preserving  infrastructure that keeps data and models under domestic or regional control, and governance  frameworks grounded in local realities. The session focuses on concrete, transferable practices: how edge-AI and privacy-enhancing  computation can strengthen domestic control over cloud infrastructure; how AI red-teaming and  security operations expertise can be grown regionally; and how ambassador and chapter-based  networks can translate global governance debates into local policy influence. The panel will also  address the specific barriers Global South institutions face <U+2014> talent retention, funding models,  and limited input into the technical standards that get set globally <U+2014> and identify forms of South- South cooperation that could accelerate progress on these gaps.  The goal is to ground the conversation on AI governance in the Global South in the technical and  institutional foundations that make governance principles enforceable in practice. The session  aims to produce concrete takeaways for the Global Dialogue on how safety and security capacity  building should be prioritised and resourced going forward. Speakers and Format This is a moderated panel discussion, followed by open Q&A with the audience. Moderator: Agustina Brizio, Coordinadora de Innovaci<U+00F3>n y Tecnolog<U+00ED>as Digitales, Asuntos del Sur  (Latin America). Speakers: Steven Guerrero (Costa Rica) <U+2014> Vice President of FAIR, the Costa Rican Foundation for  Responsible AI, and Chapter Lead for the AI Costa Rica Chapter of the Global Council for  Responsible AI. He also teaches responsible AI and digital transformation at Cenfotec University,  focusing on strengthening Latin America's institutional and human capacities to adopt, evaluate,  and govern AI systems responsibly. Michael Michie (Kenya) <U+2014> co-founder of Everse Technology Africa, a Nairobi-based venture  building edge-AI and privacy-enhancing computation for regulated industries, enabling  institutions to train and audit models on sensitive data while keeping that data resident locally.  He is also active on Africa's AI policy advisory community. Shiphrah Wairima (Kenya) <U+2014> President of the Kenya Chapter of the Global Council for  Responsible AI, and a cybersecurity leader with a background in security operations and AI red- teaming, focused on building security frameworks tailored to African threat contexts and  expanding inclusive participation in AI governance. The 60-minute format opens with brief framing remarks from each speaker (5 minutes each),  continues with a moderated cross-regional discussion connecting Latin American and African  experiences, and closes with 15<U+2013>20 minutes of audience Q&A.
