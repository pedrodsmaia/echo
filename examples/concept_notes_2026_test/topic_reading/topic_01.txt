CONCEPT NOTE    First Session of the Global Dialogue on AI Governance  6<U+2013>7 July 2026 I Palexpo, Geneva    CISG Smart Contracts: AI Governance and Trade      Date & Time:  Tuesday 7 July 2026, 12:45<U+2013>13:30 CEST, Room G    Organizers:  Tecnol<U+00F3>gico de Monterrey    Background and focus  Artificial intelligence is transforming international trade by supporting the drafting, analysis, and  execution of commercial agreements. While these technologies improve efficiency and  automation, they also raise governance questions regarding legal compliance, accountability,  transparency, and the role of human oversight. In legally sensitive domains such as international  commercial contracting, technological innovation alone is insufficient. Effective AI governance  therefore requires appropriate legal frameworks and meaningful human oversight throughout the  AI-assisted decision-making process.   This session presents research on the development and validation of an artificial intelligence (AI)  system trained on the United Nations Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods  (CISG) to assist in drafting legally compliant international sales contracts and translating them into  executable blockchain smart contracts. The study demonstrates the feasibility of using AI to  support legally compliant contract drafting and smart contract generation while revealing both the  opportunities and limitations of AI-assisted legal contracting. Although the AI system automated  substantial aspects of contract drafting and smart contract generation, it could not independently  perform complex legal reasoning, regulatory interpretation, contractual negotiations, dispute  resolution, financial verification, or legal enforcement, demonstrating that human expertise and  institutional oversight remain indispensable.   Building on these findings, the session examines their implications for AI governance in  international trade by addressing a central question: Can AI independently support the complete  lifecycle of international legal contracting? Drawing on this case study, the session argues that  effective AI-assisted legal contracting continues to depend on legal frameworks, human expertise,  financial institutions, arbitration mechanisms, and judicial enforcement working alongside AI  systems. The session concludes by presenting a preliminary discussion framework that organizes   these legal, institutional, and human oversight mechanisms to support future international  discussions on responsible AI governance for AI-assisted legal contracting.    Speakers and Format  The session will be presented by Hatem Mabrouk, Professor of International Business at  Tecnol<U+00F3>gico de Monterrey. His research focuses on international trade law, blockchain  technologies, artificial intelligence, and AI governance, with particular emphasis on the application  of emerging technologies to international business law. The presentation is based on recent  research examining whether an AI system trained on the United Nations Convention on Contracts  for the International Sale of Goods (CISG) can draft legally compliant international sales contracts  and translate them into blockchain smart contracts while identifying the practical and legal  limitations of AI-assisted legal contracting.   The session will be delivered as an expert presentation followed by a moderated discussion with  participants. It will present the research methodology, principal findings, and governance  implications while encouraging dialogue among policymakers, legal practitioners, researchers,  industry representatives, and technology experts. Participants will be invited to exchange  perspectives on the opportunities and limitations of AI-assisted legal contracting and on the legal,  institutional, and human oversight mechanisms that remain essential for responsible AI  governance. CONCEPT NOTE    First Session of the Global Dialogue on AI Governance  6<U+2013>7 July 2026 I Palexpo, Geneva    The Red Line Challenge  Global Convergence on AI Prohibitions      Date & Time:  7 July 2026, 19:00<U+2013>20:00 CEST  Organizers:  Center for AI and Digital Policy  Format: Online panel discussion  Zoom registration link: https://us02web.zoom.us/webinar/register/WN_1sIZlr1_TO-F3OHk9xSj0w  Short description for public program listing:  Enforcing prohibitions for AI systems incompatible with human rights and democratic values is a global  governance priority. Drawing on international AI frameworks and the CAIDP AI Index findings, the session  examines progress on AI prohibitions, focusing on progress, implementation gaps, and emerging issues.  Key questions include: Which AI systems are currently prohibited and how are these prohibitions  enforced? What new issues have emerged? How do we maintain momentum on implementation? Insights  can inform the UN International Scientific Panel and the Global Dialogue.  Background and focus  As AI systems become increasingly integrated into societies, governments face a fundamental governance  challenge: determining where clear red lines must be drawn to prevent AI uses that are incompatible with  human rights and democratic values. While global AI governance discussions have produced a growing  number of legal frameworks, an urgent question remains: are there circumstances where mitigation and  oversight are insufficient<U+2014>and where certain AI applications should not be permitted?  Building on the Center for AI and Digital Policy<U+2019>s (CAIDP) work on AI red lines and its CAIDP AI Index, this  session will explore the emerging global consensus that some AI uses require clear prohibitions. Across  international frameworks, including the UNESCO Recommendation on AI Ethics and the Universal  Guidelines for AI, national legislation, court decisions, and debates on military AI, governments and  institutions are increasingly recognizing that certain applications<U+2014>including those involving mass  surveillance, social scoring, manipulative systems, discriminatory profiling, and AI systems that   undermine meaningful human control<U+2014>pose risks that cannot be adequately addressed through technical  measures.  The session will examine how a human-centric approach can serve as the foundation for identifying AI red  lines. Rather than beginning with technological capabilities or existing mitigation tools, the discussion will  center on fundamental questions: What protects human dignity and agency? What safeguards equality,  democratic participation, and the rule of law?   Drawing from comparative insights across more than 90 countries through the CAIDP AI Index, the  session will examine progress in AI red lines, implementation gaps, and practical pathways for translating  principles into enforceable prohibitions. It will also consider how Global South governments and civil  society actors can shape AI governance debates as active participants in defining global norms.  The discussion will contribute to ongoing global AI governance efforts, including by providing insights  relevant to the Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, and will support a shift from identifying  risks to advancing concrete governance action. CONCEPT NOTE  First Session of the Global Dialogue on AI Governance  6<U+2013>7 July 2026 I Palexpo, Geneva    The Algorithms That Bedevil African AI Workers    Date & Time:  Monday 6 July 2026, 13:30<U+2013>14:15 CEST <U+00B7> Room G (capacity 100) <U+00B7> Event P-7 (ID 260)    Organizers:  Africa AI Policy Lab (Lawyers Hub), lead <U+00B7> London School of Economics and Political Science <U+00B7> International Labour  Organization <U+00B7> Kenya Union of Gig Workers (KUGWO) <U+00B7> Macmillan Keck, Attorneys & Solicitors <U+00B7> Government of India (IndiaAI)  Background and focus  African data labellers, content moderators, and platform workers train and maintain the systems the world now calls artificial  intelligence. They sit at the base of the global AI supply chain and work under algorithmic management they cannot see, query, or  contest. Documented cases across the continent show sub-poverty pay, traumatic exposure to harmful content, and accounts  deactivated by automated systems without human review or recourse. When scrutiny rises in one jurisdiction, the work moves to  another, and the platform-as-contractor model keeps the firm at arm<U+2019>s length from any duty of care.  The ground shifted in June 2026, when the International Labour Conference adopted Convention No. 193 on decent work in the  platform economy, the first binding international standard written for this work. It covers platform workers whether or not they hold  an employment relationship, sets safeguards on algorithmic management and on suspension and deactivation, ties employment status  to factual reality and not to contract wording, and pulls intermediaries and cross-border operators inside its scope. The standard now  exists. African ratification and the national measures that turn text into protection do not yet, and that gap is where this session works.  The discussion turns documented harm into a working agenda, asking what ratification and a national rulebook for Convention No.  193 should look like across African AI strategy and continental instruments, with a standing cross-partner group to carry the drafting  towards the 2027 Dialogue.  Speakers and Format  A 45-minute panel that will highlight African worker experience at the global dialogue and member states/institutions that can act.  The session chair will open with framing, and worker testimonies will anchor the discussion. Adam Lederer and Kenneth Agengo  then present the evidence together: a short documentary on AI worker rights and concerns alongside the gig-worker findings of the  Africa AI Governance Index, launched on the sidelines of the Summit on 5 July 2026.
